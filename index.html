Pedagogical Guide: Exploring Fitness Landscapes

1. Why Begin with this Experiment?

When introducing Genetic Algorithms (GAs) to researchers with mathematical or computational backgrounds who are new to evolutionary metaheuristics, the most frequent misconception is: "Why not simply evaluate points at random (Monte Carlo) or apply gradient descent?"

This simulator is designed to give students a direct, hands-on experience of the curse of dimensionality and the deceptiveness of complex multimodal landscapes. In real-world black-box optimization, the evaluator has no access to analytic equations or terrain topography: one can only propose a solution vector $\mathbf{x}$ and receive a scalar feedback value (fitness).

2. Schaffer's F6 Function

Introduced by J. David Schaffer in 1989, this benchmark is a classic milestone in evolutionary computation literature:

f(x, y) = 0.5 - \frac{\sin^2\left(\sqrt{(x-x_0)^2 + (y-y_0)^2}\right) - 0.5}{\left[1 + 0.001\cdot\left((x-x_0)^2 + (y-y_0)^2\right)\right]^2}

Key Mathematical Properties:

  • Infinite Concentric Local Optima: The oscillatory term $\sin^2(r)$ creates continuous concentric ridges and valleys that trap gradient methods and local hill-climbers.
  • Microscopic Global Peak: The true maximum peak ($f^* = 1.0$) is located strictly at the center $(x_0, y_0)$, with an effective radius smaller than $1.57$ units. In a standard search space of $[-100, 100] \times [-100, 100]$ (area of $40,000$ square units), the peak occupies less than 0.019% of the total domain. In an expanded space of $[-500, 500]$ (area $1,000,000$), it occupies less than 0.00077%!
  • Deceptive Asymptotic Plateau: Far away from the center, the denominator dampens oscillations toward an asymptotic value of $0.5$. Blind sampling perceives a nearly flat, featureless plain offering zero guidance on where the global basin is situated.

3. Student Exercise Dynamics

  1. Manual Blind Sampling: With the center set to Hidden Random, attempt to click 10 times on the map to find the optimum. Notice how difficult it is to reach even 60% fitness.
  2. Brute Force Random Sampling (Monte Carlo): Inject 100 or 200 random points. Notice that despite consuming hundreds of costly function evaluations, the best sample rarely exceeds 75-80% fitness, especially in larger search domains.
  3. The Power of Genetic Algorithms: Click the GA Evolution button. Observe how selection pressure and genetic crossover exploit accumulated population information to rapidly converge toward 99%+ with a small fraction of the computational budget.

4. Module 2: The N-Queens Problem & Combinatorial Optimization

The N-Queens problem requires placing $N$ chess queens on an $N \times N$ chessboard such that no two queens threaten each other (i.e. no two queens share the same row, column, or diagonal).

\text{Diagonal Clash Condition between } (r_1, c_1) \text{ and } (r_2, c_2): \quad |r_1 - r_2| = |c_1 - c_2|

Search Space Complexity & Chromosomal Encoding:

  • 1D/2D Binary Vector (0s and 1s): A bitstream of $N^2$ bits where each bit represents a square ($1$ if occupied by a queen, $0$ if empty). For 8 queens this yields 64 bits with $2^{64} \approx 1.84 \times 10^{19}$ possible configurations.
  • Permutation Representation (Column Phenotype): By strictly enforcing exactly one queen per column and row, the search space collapses drastically from $N^N$ ($16^{16} \approx 1.84 \times 10^{19}$) down to $N!$ ($16! \approx 2.09 \times 10^{13}$). The Genetic Algorithm then focuses exclusively on eliminating diagonal conflicts using specialized genetic operators such as Order Crossover (OX) and swap/inversion mutations.
